The Dinner Timing Guide

Cooking four things is not hard. Having four things ready at seven o’clock is. The answer is a few minutes with a pencil before the oven goes on.

Work backwards from the table

Every dish has two numbers: how long it cooks and how long it rests. Add them together, subtract from the time you want to eat, and you have its start time. Do that for each dish and write the start times in order. That list is your whole plan.

An example for 7:00 pm

Roast chicken, 80 minutes plus 15 resting: in at 5:25. Roast potatoes, 50 minutes: in at 6:10. Gravy, 10 minutes: start at 6:50, using the resting juices. Green beans, 8 minutes: on at 6:52. The Dinner Timing Planner builds this list for any menu.

Add what recipes leave out

Recipe times begin when the food goes in. Real time begins earlier. Build these into the plan:

  • Preheating: most ovens need fifteen minutes to reach temperature.
  • Boiling water: a large pan takes ten to twelve minutes from cold.
  • Preparation: peeling and chopping for four takes fifteen to twenty minutes if you are unhurried.
  • Carving and plating: allow five minutes that nobody ever allows.

Let resting do the work

Resting is not dead time. A roast that rests for twenty minutes frees the oven at exactly the moment potatoes need a blast of high heat, and frees your hands for gravy and greens. Plan the last quarter of an hour around the resting meat and the whole meal becomes calmer.

Cover resting meat loosely with foil. Wrapped tightly it steams and loses its crisp skin.

Choose a menu that staggers itself

The easiest dinners to time use different parts of the kitchen. One dish in the oven, one on the hob, one that needs no heat at all. Trouble starts when three things need the hob in the same five minutes.

  • Pair a roast with one roasted side and one quick green vegetable.
  • Serve a salad or a cold dressed vegetable alongside anything complicated.
  • Pick a starch that can wait: rice sits happily off the heat for ten minutes with the lid on.

Holding food warm

Sooner or later something will be ready early. Most cooked food holds well for twenty to thirty minutes in a low oven, around 70°C, covered. Mash stays hot in a bowl over a pan of barely simmering water. Sauces keep in a small pan with a lid, off the heat, and are brought back to a simmer at the last moment. Green vegetables do not hold; cook those last.

When a dish runs late

If the main is behind, do not rush it by turning the heat up. Turn everything else down or off, cover it, and wait. A dinner served fifteen minutes late is forgotten by the second mouthful. An undercooked chicken is not. For poultry and for reheated food, check that it is piping hot all the way through before serving.

Write it down

Put the list of start times on a scrap of paper by the cooker and set a timer for each one. Experienced cooks do this for a large meal, and it is the single habit that makes timing look effortless.
